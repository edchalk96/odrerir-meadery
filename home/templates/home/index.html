History of Mead

Ancient Origins & Etymology
Long before the Vikings, mead was humanity's first ferment—accidental wild yeast fermenting rainwater in beehives. Residues in China date back to 7000 BCE, and ancient Greeks hailed it as the "nectar of the gods." Contrary to myth, the Norse didn't invent the word mead; it stems from the ancient root *médʰu, which evolved into Old Norse mjǫðr and, ultimately, English mead.
The Mead of Poetry & "Magic Sticks"
The Norse elevated honey-wine into a divine legend. Myth tells that the wisdom of the gods was brewed into the Mead of Poetry (Óðrœrir), stolen by Odin to bestow eloquence upon mortals. In reality, Viking brewing relied on a wooden stirring stick (kveikstokk). Believed to hold magical spirits that turned honey into alcohol, the porous wood was actually inoculated with wild yeast strains, passed down as cherished heirlooms.
Valhalla, Honeymoons & Cultural Status
Due to the scarcity of honey in cold northern climates, mead was a rare luxury signaling honor and hospitality. Fallen warriors in Valhalla were promised endless horns of mead served by the mythical goat Heiðrún. Closer to home, newlyweds received a full moon's supply of honey-wine to celebrate their marriage—a traditional custom widely credited as the origin of the word "honeymoon."
The Modern Renaissance
Though large-scale agriculture eventually pushed mead into the background in favor of cheaper beer and wine, honey-wine is experiencing a global renaissance today. Driven by modern craft beverage movements and a renewed appreciation for historic brewing, mead has returned to its rightful place as an artisanal, hand-forged drink.
